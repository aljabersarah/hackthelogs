Email Security Gateway Logs

Email Security Log Overview

Email remains the most common route into an organisation. Phishing for credentials, malicious attachments, and business email compromise together account for the majority of initial access in real intrusions, which makes the email security gateway one of the highest value log sources a SOC can collect. Unlike most controls, it sees the attack before it succeeds: the message arrives, is scanned, and is delivered or blocked, and every one of those decisions is recorded with the sender, the recipient, the subject, the attachment hashes, and the URLs contained in the body.

Three distinct record types matter, and they are frequently confused. Message tracking records the journey of every message through the mail flow, whether malicious or not, and is what lets you answer who else received a phishing email. Verdict and quarantine records document what the gateway decided and why, including the specific detection that fired. Post-delivery records cover what happened after the message landed: whether a user clicked a link, whether an attachment was opened, and whether the platform later retracted the message once new intelligence arrived.

That last category is the one most often overlooked and the most operationally important. Attackers routinely send a benign link that is weaponised hours later, precisely because the message passes inspection at delivery time. Microsoft's Safe Links and equivalent features from Proofpoint and Mimecast rewrite URLs so that each click is evaluated at click time rather than delivery time, and the resulting click logs are frequently the only evidence that a user actually engaged with a phishing message. Zero-hour Auto Purge and its equivalents then retroactively remove messages from mailboxes, and those retraction events tell you a threat reached users before it was recognised.

Key Email Security Log Types

An email security platform produces several streams covering delivery, verdicts, and user interaction:

Log Type Description
Message Tracking Log One record per message per hop, containing the envelope sender, recipients, subject, size, message identifier, and the action taken. The foundation of every phishing investigation because it answers who else received the same message.
Threat Verdict Log The security decision for each message: spam, phish, malware, or clean, with the confidence level and the specific detection technology that fired. Distinguishes a bulk marketing block from a targeted credential phish.
URL Click Log Records each time a user clicks a rewritten link, with the verdict at click time and whether the user proceeded past a warning page. Frequently the only proof that a user actually engaged with a phishing message.
Attachment Detonation Log Sandbox analysis results for attachments, including file hashes, observed behaviour, and the verdict. Provides the hashes needed to hunt for the same file elsewhere in the estate.
Authentication Results SPF, DKIM, DMARC, and ARC evaluation for each inbound message. The basis for detecting domain spoofing and for measuring whether your own outbound authentication is correctly configured.
Quarantine and Release Log Messages held, and critically, messages released by administrators or users. A released phishing message is a control that was overridden and warrants review.
Post-Delivery Events Retroactive actions such as Zero-hour Auto Purge removing a message from mailboxes after delivery. Each one means a threat reached users before it was recognised.
Mail Flow Rule and DLP Log Transport rule matches and data loss prevention verdicts on outbound mail. Detects both policy violations and attacker-created forwarding rules.
Outbound Spam Log Messages sent from your tenant that were flagged as spam or phishing. Almost always the sign of an internally compromised account being used to attack others.
Pro Tip: Outbound spam detection is the most under-monitored email log. When your own users start sending phishing, the account is compromised and the attacker is using your domain reputation to attack partners and customers. Alert on it as an account compromise indicator, not as a mail hygiene issue.

Configuring Email Security Logging

Several of the most valuable email logs require explicit enabling. These steps cover the common gaps.

Step 1: Enable Safe Links Click Tracking

Connect-ExchangeOnline New-SafeLinksPolicy -Name "HTL-SafeLinks" ` -EnableSafeLinksForEmail $true ` -EnableSafeLinksForTeams $true ` -EnableSafeLinksForOffice $true ` -TrackClicks $true ` -AllowClickThrough $false ` -ScanUrls $true ` -DeliverMessageAfterScan $true ` -EnableForInternalSenders $true New-SafeLinksRule -Name "HTL-SafeLinks-All" ` -SafeLinksPolicy "HTL-SafeLinks" -RecipientDomainIs corp.com
TrackClicks is what produces the click log. AllowClickThrough set to false prevents users bypassing the warning page, and EnableForInternalSenders catches phishing sent from already-compromised internal accounts.

Step 2: Enable Safe Attachments Detonation

New-SafeAttachmentPolicy -Name "HTL-SafeAttach" ` -Enable $true -Action Block -Redirect $false ` -QuarantineTag DefaultFullAccessPolicy New-SafeAttachmentRule -Name "HTL-SafeAttach-All" ` -SafeAttachmentPolicy "HTL-SafeAttach" -RecipientDomainIs corp.com # Dynamic Delivery avoids the delay while detonation completes Set-SafeAttachmentPolicy -Identity "HTL-SafeAttach" -Action DynamicDelivery
Detonation produces the file hashes that let you hunt for the same attachment elsewhere. Dynamic Delivery delivers the body immediately and attaches the file once analysis finishes, removing the usual objection to enabling this.

Step 3: Enforce Inbound Authentication Checks

# Publish your own DMARC record in DNS # _dmarc.corp.com TXT "v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc@corp.com; pct=100" # Honour DMARC on inbound mail rather than ignoring it Set-AntiPhishPolicy -Identity "Office365 AntiPhish Default" ` -HonorDmarcPolicy $true ` -DmarcRejectAction Reject ` -DmarcQuarantineAction Quarantine ` -EnableSpoofIntelligence $true ` -EnableUnauthenticatedSender $true ` -EnableViaTag $true
The unauthenticated sender indicator and the via tag are user-visible warnings on messages that fail authentication. Publishing your own DMARC record at p=reject stops others being phished using your domain.

Step 4: Configure Impersonation Protection

New-AntiPhishPolicy -Name "HTL-AntiPhish" ` -EnableTargetedUserProtection $true ` -TargetedUsersToProtect "Sarah Aljaber;s.aljaber@corp.com","CFO Name;cfo@corp.com" ` -EnableTargetedDomainsProtection $true ` -TargetedDomainsToProtect corp.com,corp-group.com ` -EnableMailboxIntelligence $true ` -EnableMailboxIntelligenceProtection $true ` -TargetedUserProtectionAction Quarantine ` -PhishThresholdLevel 3
Impersonation protection is what catches business email compromise. Protect executives, finance staff, and anyone with payment authority by name, because these attacks target roles rather than systems.

Step 5: Enable Mailbox and Outbound Auditing

Set-OrganizationConfig -AuditDisabled $false Get-Mailbox -ResultSize Unlimited | Set-Mailbox -AuditEnabled $true ` -AuditOwner @{Add="MailItemsAccessed","Send","SoftDelete","HardDelete","UpdateInboxRules"} # Alert on outbound spam, which signals internal compromise Set-HostedOutboundSpamFilterPolicy -Identity Default ` -BccSuspiciousOutboundMail $true ` -NotifyOutboundSpam $true ` -NotifyOutboundSpamRecipients soc@corp.com ` -AutoForwardingMode Off
Setting AutoForwardingMode to Off blocks the external forwarding rules attackers create to maintain visibility of a mailbox after losing access to it.

Step 6: Export Logs to a SIEM

# Office 365 Management Activity API subscriptions POST /api/v1.0/{tenant}/activity/feed/subscriptions/start?contentType=Audit.Exchange POST /api/v1.0/{tenant}/activity/feed/subscriptions/start?contentType=Audit.General POST /api/v1.0/{tenant}/activity/feed/subscriptions/start?contentType=DLP.All # Or stream Defender hunting tables into Sentinel # Microsoft 365 Defender connector, tables: # EmailEvents, EmailUrlInfo, EmailAttachmentInfo, # EmailPostDeliveryEvents, UrlClickEvents
Message Trace holds 90 days and hunting tables hold 30. Continuous export is the only way to answer questions about a phishing campaign that began months earlier.
Security Note: Email logs contain subjects, sender and recipient addresses, and often attachment names. That is sensitive business and personal data subject to the same regulatory obligations as the mailboxes themselves. Restrict access and set retention deliberately rather than by storage cost alone.

Email Threat Patterns

Email attacks divide into those carrying a payload and those carrying only words. The second kind defeats most technical controls.

Credential Phishing

A link to a fake login page harvesting credentials. Signature: newly registered sender domain, urgency in the subject, and a URL whose display text does not match its destination.
Detect Critical

Business Email Compromise

No link and no attachment, just a request to change payment details or make a transfer. Nothing for a scanner to detect, so display name spoofing and reply-to mismatch are the only signals.
Detect Critical

Delayed Weaponisation

A benign link that becomes malicious hours after delivery, defeating inspection at delivery time. Only click-time evaluation and post-delivery retraction catch this.
Detect Critical

Thread Hijacking

Replying into a genuine existing conversation from a compromised account. Extremely convincing because the context is real, and the sender is legitimately who they claim to be.
Detect Critical

Malicious Attachments

Container formats such as ISO, IMG, and password-protected archives that evade scanning, or documents with embedded scripts. Detonation verdicts and file hashes are the detection.
Detect High Risk

Malicious Inbox Rules

Post-compromise persistence: rules that forward mail externally or delete replies so the victim never sees the fraud they are part of. Often named with a single character.
Detect Critical
Security Note: Business email compromise carries no payload at all, which means attachment sandboxing and URL rewriting are irrelevant to it. The only technical signals are envelope versus header sender mismatch, reply-to pointing elsewhere, lookalike domains, and a display name matching a real executive from an unfamiliar address.

Authentication Results and Verdicts

Email authentication results are the most reliable structured signal available on inbound mail. These are the values worth building detections around.

Result Meaning Why It Matters
SPF fail Sending server not authorised by the domain Direct domain spoofing, though forwarding legitimately breaks SPF so it cannot be used alone
DKIM fail Message signature invalid or absent The message was altered in transit or was never signed by the claimed domain
DMARC fail Neither SPF nor DKIM aligned with the header From domain The strongest single spoofing indicator, because alignment is what SPF and DKIM alone do not check
compauth fail Microsoft composite authentication failed Combines all signals plus sender reputation and history. A fail with reason 000 or 001 means implicit authentication failed outright
ARC pass Authenticated Received Chain intact Preserves original authentication results through legitimate forwarding, reducing false positives from mailing lists
Spoof intelligence Sender spoofing an internal or known domain Detects the case where an external server sends mail claiming to be from your own domain
Impersonation domain Sender domain visually similar to a protected one Lookalike domains such as corp-invoices.top or rn instead of m. The primary business email compromise vector
Mailbox intelligence Sender unusual for this specific recipient Uses each user's own communication history, catching first contact from an unfamiliar party mid-conversation
ZAP removed Message retracted after delivery A threat reached the mailbox before it was recognised. Check whether the user interacted before removal
ClickedThrough User proceeded past a block warning The user was warned and continued anyway. Treat as a confirmed compromise until proven otherwise
Pro Tip: Never alert on SPF failure alone. Legitimate forwarding, mailing lists, and third party senders break SPF constantly. DMARC failure combined with a composite authentication failure is far more precise, because alignment and reputation are evaluated together.

Email Security Log Analysis Queries

These KQL and PowerShell queries cover phishing campaigns, business email compromise, and post-compromise mailbox activity.

Users Who Clicked Through a Block
KQL> UrlClickEvents | where ActionType == "ClickAllowed" or IsClickedThrough == 1 | project Timestamp, AccountUpn, Url, ThreatTypes, IPAddress | order by Timestamp desc
The highest priority email query. These users saw a warning page and continued anyway, so treat each as a probable credential compromise.
Full Campaign Scope
KQL> EmailEvents | where SenderFromDomain == "corp-invoices.top" or Subject has "Updated Bank Details" | project Timestamp, RecipientEmailAddress, DeliveryAction, DeliveryLocation, NetworkMessageId | order by Timestamp asc
The first question in any phishing investigation is who else received it. Pivot on sender domain, subject, or attachment hash to find every copy.
Executive Impersonation
KQL> EmailEvents | where EmailDirection == "Inbound" | where SenderDisplayName has_any ("Sarah Aljaber","CFO","Chief Financial") | where SenderFromDomain != "corp.com" | project Timestamp, SenderDisplayName, SenderFromAddress, RecipientEmailAddress, Subject
An external sender using an executive display name is business email compromise. Catches the attack even when no link or attachment is present.
Reply-To Mismatch
KQL> EmailEvents | where EmailDirection == "Inbound" and isnotempty(ReplyToAddress) | extend replyDomain = tostring(split(ReplyToAddress,"@")[1]) | where replyDomain != SenderFromDomain | project Timestamp, SenderFromAddress, ReplyToAddress, Subject, RecipientEmailAddress
A reply-to pointing at a different domain than the sender routes the victim's response to the attacker. One of the highest fidelity BEC signals available.
Malicious Inbox Rules
KQL> OfficeActivity | where Operation in ("New-InboxRule","Set-InboxRule") | where Parameters has_any ("ForwardTo","RedirectTo","ForwardAsAttachmentTo","DeleteMessage","MarkAsRead") | project TimeGenerated, UserId, ClientIP, Parameters
Rules forwarding externally or auto-deleting are post-compromise persistence and concealment. Frequently named with a single dot or space to hide in the UI.
Outbound Spam (Internal Compromise)
KQL> EmailEvents | where EmailDirection == "Outbound" and ThreatTypes has_any ("Spam","Phish") | summarize count(), dc(RecipientEmailAddress) as targets by SenderFromAddress | where targets > 10
Your own users sending phishing means their accounts are compromised and your domain reputation is being spent attacking partners.
Post-Delivery Retraction
KQL> EmailPostDeliveryEvents | where ActionType startswith "Zap" | join kind=leftouter (UrlClickEvents) on NetworkMessageId | project Timestamp, RecipientEmailAddress, ActionType, ActionResult, Url, IsClickedThrough
Joining retraction against clicks answers the critical question: did the user interact before the message was pulled back?
Quarantine Releases
PS> Get-QuarantineMessage -StartReceivedDate (Get-Date).AddDays(-7) -ReleaseStatus RELEASED | Select-Object ReceivedTime, SenderAddress, RecipientAddress, Subject, Type, ReleasedUser
A released phishing message is a control that was deliberately overridden. Review who released it and why.
Authentication Failures Delivered
KQL> EmailEvents | where EmailDirection == "Inbound" and DeliveryAction == "Delivered" | where AuthenticationDetails has "DMARC='fail'" and AuthenticationDetails has "compauth=fail" | summarize count() by SenderFromDomain, SenderIPv4 | order by count_ desc
Messages that failed both DMARC and composite authentication yet still reached the inbox. Each represents a policy gap worth closing.
Attachment Hash Hunting
KQL> EmailAttachmentInfo | where SHA256 == "a1b2c3d4e5f6..." | join kind=inner EmailEvents on NetworkMessageId | project Timestamp, RecipientEmailAddress, FileName, DeliveryAction, DeliveryLocation
Detonation supplies the hash, and this query finds everyone who received the same file. Feed that hash to the endpoint platform to find where it executed.

Best Practices for Email Security Logging

Apply these practices so the most common initial access vector is also your best instrumented one:

01

Enable Click Tracking

URL rewriting with click logging is frequently the only proof a user engaged with a phishing message, and it evaluates the destination at click time rather than delivery time.

02

Export Before Retention Expires

Message Trace holds 90 days and hunting tables 30. Phishing investigations routinely reach further back, so continuous export to a SIEM is essential.

03

Monitor Outbound as Closely as Inbound

Your own users sending phishing is an account compromise, not a hygiene problem. It is also actively damaging your domain reputation with partners.

04

Configure Impersonation Protection by Name

Business email compromise carries no payload, so sandboxing and URL rewriting do nothing. Protecting executives and finance staff by name is what catches it.

05

Publish and Honour DMARC

A DMARC record at p=reject stops others being phished with your domain, and honouring inbound DMARC stops your users receiving spoofed mail. Both directions matter.

06

Alert on Inbox Rule Creation

Forwarding and auto-delete rules are the standard post-compromise persistence and concealment technique, and they are low volume enough to review individually.

07

Correlate Retraction Against Clicks

A retracted message tells you a threat got through. Joining it against the click log tells you whether it worked, which is the difference between an alert and an incident.

08

Never Alert on SPF Alone

Legitimate forwarding breaks SPF constantly. Combine DMARC failure with composite authentication failure for a signal precise enough to act on.

Common Email Security Log Examples

A phishing campaign from delivery through credential theft to fraudulent payment.

Credential Phish Delivered to the Inbox

Timestamp: 2025-07-14T09:12:04Z NetworkMessageId: 8f2a1c04-1122-4e91-b7d2-0010f4e21b00 SenderFromAddress: it-support@corp-helpdesk.top SenderMailFromAddress: bounce@mailer-9f2a.top SenderDisplayName: Corp IT Service Desk SenderIPv4: 203.0.113.44 RecipientEmailAddress: r.almutairi@corp.com Subject: Action Required: Your password expires in 24 hours ThreatTypes: (empty) DeliveryAction: Delivered DeliveryLocation: Inbox AuthenticationDetails: SPF=pass; DKIM=pass; DMARC=none; compauth=pass
Authentication passed because the attacker owns corp-helpdesk.top and configured it correctly. Authentication proves who sent it, never whether they are trustworthy. The lookalike domain is the real signal.

User Clicks the Rewritten Link

Timestamp: 2025-07-14T09:38:02Z AccountUpn: r.almutairi@corp.com Url: https://corp-helpdesk.top/sso/login?id=a7f3c19e ActionType: ClickAllowed IsClickedThrough: 1 ThreatTypes: Phish IPAddress: 10.30.2.61 NetworkMessageId: 8f2a1c04-1122-4e91-b7d2-0010f4e21b00
The verdict changed to Phish between delivery and click, so the warning page appeared. IsClickedThrough of 1 means the user read the warning and proceeded regardless.

Retroactive Removal After the Fact

Timestamp: 2025-07-14T10:04:55Z ActionType: ZapPhish ActionResult: Success RecipientEmailAddress: r.almutairi@corp.com NetworkMessageId: 8f2a1c04-1122-4e91-b7d2-0010f4e21b00 ActionTrigger: Threat intelligence update
The message was pulled from the mailbox 52 minutes after the user had already clicked. Retraction proves a threat got through; only the click log proves it succeeded.

Campaign Scope Across the Organisation

SenderFromDomain: corp-helpdesk.top Delivered to Inbox : 14 recipients Delivered to Junk : 31 recipients Blocked : 88 recipients Clicked link : 3 recipients Clicked through warning: 1 recipient First seen: 2025-07-14T09:08:44Z Last seen: 2025-07-14T09:22:19Z
133 recipients targeted in 14 minutes, 14 reached the inbox, and one user proceeded past the warning. This summary drives both the containment scope and the awareness follow-up.

Business Email Compromise with No Payload

Timestamp: 2025-07-14T10:18:12Z SenderFromAddress: s.aljaber@corp-group.co SenderDisplayName: Sarah Aljaber ReplyToAddress: accounts.sarah@mailfence-secure.top RecipientEmailAddress: finance@corp.com Subject: RE: Q3 Supplier Payment Schedule ThreatTypes: Phish DetectionMethods: Impersonation domain, Mailbox intelligence DeliveryAction: Quarantined AuthenticationDetails: SPF=pass; DKIM=pass; DMARC=pass; compauth=pass
Every authentication check passed. It was caught by impersonation protection noticing corp-group.co against the real corp-group.com, and by the reply-to pointing at a third domain entirely.

Malicious Attachment in a Container Format

Timestamp: 2025-07-14T09:47:33Z FileName: Invoice_July_2025.iso FileType: iso FileSize: 1284119 SHA256: a1b2c3d4e5f6789012345678901234567890abcdef1234567890abcdef123456 ThreatTypes: Malware DetectionMethods: Safe Attachments detonation DetonationVerdict: Malicious DetonationBehaviours: Creates LNK on mount, spawns rundll32.exe, contacts 203.0.113.91 DeliveryAction: Blocked
ISO files mount as drives and bypass mark-of-the-web, which is why they replaced macro documents. The behaviours recorded here give the endpoint team exactly what to hunt for.

Post-Compromise Inbox Rule

Operation: New-InboxRule UserId: r.almutairi@corp.com ClientIP: 203.0.113.44 Parameters: Name : "." From : finance@corp.com; ap@corp.com ForwardTo : archive-9f2a@mailfence-secure.top DeleteMessage : True StopProcessingRules : True
A rule named with a single dot so it is nearly invisible in Outlook, forwarding finance correspondence externally and deleting the local copy so the victim never sees the replies.

Outbound Phishing from the Compromised Account

Timestamp: 2025-07-14T11:02:44Z EmailDirection: Outbound SenderFromAddress: r.almutairi@corp.com RecipientEmailAddress: [42 external recipients across 18 partner domains] Subject: RE: Shared document for your review ThreatTypes: Phish DetectionMethods: URL reputation DeliveryAction: Blocked Reason: Outbound spam policy
The compromised account now attacks partners from a genuine, authenticated corp.com address, replying into real threads. Outbound blocking caught it, and that block is the clearest possible account compromise alert.
